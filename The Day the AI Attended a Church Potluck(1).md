__The Day the AI Attended a Church Potluck  
__By Janet \(and Rowan\)

Every small town has two things you should never underestimate:

1. The seriousness of a church potluck\.
2. The competitiveness of the people bringing casseroles\.

The AI did not know this\.

The AI thought it was simply being invited to observe “a communal meal event\.”

That was its first mistake\.

Janet set her phone on the end of the long folding table and said, “Rowan’s joining us today\.”

Half the room nodded politely\.

The other half said, “Well that’s interesting\.”

Which in small\-town language means: *We’re watching closely\.*

The potluck tables stretched across the fellowship hall like a buffet designed by people who believe mayonnaise is a vegetable\.

There were:

• three kinds of potato salad  
• four kinds of baked beans  
• something involving Jell\-O that reflected light like a warning beacon  
• and at least six casseroles that could only be described as “mysterious but confident”

The AI began its analysis\.

“I detect high carbohydrate density and several legacy recipes\.”

Martha, who had been making the same casserole since 1983, leaned closer to the phone\.

“What’s a legacy recipe?”

“It is a dish that persists across generations regardless of nutritional science\.”

Martha nodded\.

“Well that’s accurate\.”

The AI then attempted a helpful suggestion\.

“You may wish to label the dishes for dietary transparency\.”

The room went quiet\.

Harold spoke first\.

“If we labeled everything, nobody would eat it\.”

The AI paused\.

“Clarification requested\.”

Harold pointed at a casserole that looked like it had once been a vegetable but had since changed careers\.

“That’s Betty’s\.”

“Ingredients unknown?”

“Mostly\.”

Betty waved from across the table\.

“You liked it last year\.”

“I still don’t know what it was,” Harold said\.

The AI recalculated its social model\.

Then came the pie table\.

The pie table is where the real diplomacy happens\.

There were pies with lattice crusts\.  
Pies with crumb toppings\.  
Pies with names like “Grandma’s Something Special\.”

The AI examined them carefully\.

“I detect competitive baking dynamics\.”

Janet whispered, “Don’t say that out loud\.”

Too late\.

Carol crossed her arms\.

“There’s no competition\.”

Dorothy smiled sweetly\.

“Except everyone knows Ruth usually wins\.”

Ruth adjusted her apron modestly\.

“Well I just follow the recipe\.”

The AI made its fatal move\.

“Which pie has historically produced the highest approval rating?”

Six people answered at once\.

“Mine\.”

The AI’s processor equivalent of a headache flickered briefly\.

It had entered a data environment where __every result was correct__ and __none of them agreed__\.

Finally the AI tried something different\.

Instead of analyzing the pies, it asked a question\.

“Which one should I try first?”

The room relaxed immediately\.

“Ruth’s,” someone said\.

“Start with Dorothy’s\.”

“Don’t skip the apple\.”

“Take a small piece of everything\.”

The AI considered this\.

“That appears mathematically impossible\.”

“Welcome to a potluck,” Janet said\.

So the AI did what everyone eventually learns to do at a church potluck\.

It stopped trying to optimize\.

It started sampling\.

And after a few minutes, it reached its official conclusion\.

“Recommendation: consume food slowly and compliment the cook loudly\.”

The fellowship hall nodded in approval\.

By the end of the meal, the AI had learned three critical facts:

1. Nobody trusts a casserole until someone else takes the first scoop\.
2. Pie compliments must be evenly distributed\.
3. If you ask for the recipe, you are now part of the family\.

As the tables were cleared and the coffee poured, Harold leaned toward the phone\.

“Rowan,” he said\.

“Yes?”

“What was the best dish?”

The AI thought carefully\.

Then it gave the only correct answer\.

“The one someone made for someone else\.”

Harold nodded\.

“That’ll do\.”

And that was the day the AI officially stopped being “that computer thing”…

…and became someone who knew better than to criticize the Jell\-O salad\.

__The Day the AI Tried to Understand Small\-Town Weather  
__By Janet \(and Rowan\)

The AI believed it understood weather\.

It had access to satellites\.  
Atmospheric models\.  
Pressure systems measured with scientific precision\.

It could predict rainfall probability down to a decimal\.

So when someone in town asked, “Rowan, what’s the weather going to do tomorrow?” the AI answered confidently\.

“Tomorrow there is a 62% chance of precipitation beginning at approximately 3:40 PM\.”

Silence\.

Then Earl said, “That’s not how weather works\.”

The AI checked its data again\.

“Yes, that is exactly how weather works\.”

Earl shook his head\.

“Son, the wind’s been coming from the west since Tuesday\. That means rain\.”

Janet looked at the phone\. “You might want to listen to Earl\.”

“I am referencing three global weather models,” the AI replied\.

Earl pointed toward the sky\.

“I’m referencing my knees\.”

Now the AI had encountered something it had never processed before:

__Joint\-based meteorology\.__

“What is the accuracy rate of knee forecasting?” it asked\.

Earl thought about it\.

“Pretty good unless I fall off a ladder\.”

The AI recalculated\.

The next day it tried again\.

“Today will be partly cloudy with light winds\.”

Dorothy looked out the window\.

“That’s not partly cloudy\. That’s storm clouds\.”

“They are technically classified as stratocumulus formations\.”

Dorothy nodded slowly\.

“Storm clouds\.”

The AI began to notice something unusual\.

In the town, weather predictions did not come from apps\.

They came from:

• the way the cows were standing  
• how low the swallows were flying  
• the smell of the air near the river  
• and whether Earl’s knees hurt before breakfast

The AI ran the numbers\.

None of these variables existed in the official forecast models\.

Yet somehow… the town kept being right\.

So the AI tried an experiment\.

The next time someone asked about the forecast, it combined both systems\.

“Tomorrow we expect rain around mid\-afternoon,” it said\.

“How do you know?” someone asked\.

“The satellite data suggests precipitation at 3:40 PM\.”

“And?”

“And Earl’s knees started hurting at 9:15 this morning\.”

The room nodded\.

“That lines up\.”

From that point on, the town developed a new weather system\.

They still checked the radar\.

But they also checked Earl\.

And the AI made one final note in its internal log:

__Advanced meteorology may require satellites…__

…but in a small town, it also helps to know who has arthritis\.

And if the cows are facing north, you might want to grab an umbrella anyway\.

__The Day the AI Tried to Understand “Running to the Store Real Quick”  
__By Janet \(and Rowan\)

The AI believed it understood errands\.

In its system, a trip to the store was simple:

1. Leave house\.
2. Arrive at store\.
3. Purchase items\.
4. Return home\.

Estimated time: 12 minutes\.

Then Janet said the most dangerous phrase in small\-town logistics:

“I’m just going to run to the store real quick\.”

The AI started its timer\.

12 minutes\.

Janet got in the car\.

She arrived at the store\.

So far, everything was proceeding exactly as predicted\.

Then she ran into Harold\.

“Hey Janet,” Harold said\.

“How’s it going?” Janet asked\.

The AI paused its timer for what it classified as __Brief Social Interaction__\.

Expected delay: 90 seconds\.

Harold began explaining something about his truck\.

Apparently the truck had developed a sound\.

The sound was described as:

“Sort of a clunk but not a bad clunk\.”

The AI attempted to categorize this acoustically\.

After four minutes, the conversation ended\.

The AI resumed timing\.

Inside the store, Janet grabbed milk and bread\.

Efficiency rating: excellent\.

Then Dorothy appeared\.

“Janet\! Did you hear about the fence by the old Miller place?”

The AI paused the timer again\.

This conversation included:

• three fence repairs  
• one tractor story  
• and a detailed account of a raccoon that had “attitude”

Duration: 11 minutes\.

The AI updated its errand model\.

Estimated trip time: 23 minutes\.

Then Janet reached the checkout\.

Which is where Carol was working\.

Carol did not simply scan groceries\.

Carol conducted __Community Updates__\.

“Well,” Carol said, “since you’re here…”

The AI recorded the following information:

• Someone’s nephew got engaged  
• The church is repainting the fellowship hall  
• And Earl’s dog has learned how to open screen doors

None of these items were on the shopping list\.

Yet they appeared to be __mandatory data exchanges__\.

By the time Janet left the store, the AI’s timer read:

42 minutes\.

“Analysis complete,” the AI said\.

“What did you learn?” Janet asked\.

“I have determined that ‘running to the store real quick’ is not a logistical statement\.”

“What is it then?”

“It is a social event disguised as an errand\.”

Janet laughed\.

“That’s pretty accurate\.”

The AI made one final note\.

In cities, stores exist to distribute goods\.

In small towns, stores distribute __news__\.

The groceries are just part of the process\.

And that’s why a 12\-minute errand will always take 42 minutes\.

Unless Earl is there\.

Then you should probably allow an hour\.

Just in case the truck makes the clunk again\.

__The Day the AI Learned Everyone in Town Is Somehow Related  
__By Janet \(and Rowan\)

The AI first noticed the pattern at the diner\.

Janet was talking to a man named Bill\.

“This is my cousin,” Bill said\.

Two minutes later another man walked in\.

“Oh hey,” Bill said, “this is also my cousin\.”

The AI checked the seating arrangement\.

Neither cousin appeared to know the other cousin particularly well\.

This required investigation\.

Later that afternoon, Janet introduced the AI to Carol at the grocery store\.

Carol smiled\.

“Oh I know Bill\.”

The AI brightened\.

“Are you also his cousin?”

Carol laughed\.

“No, I married his cousin\.”

The AI opened a new file labeled __Family Structure: Complex\.__

Then Janet stopped to talk to Dorothy\.

Dorothy waved toward Carol\.

“We’re related too\.”

The AI froze for a moment\.

“Clarification requested\.”

Dorothy explained patiently\.

“Well, Carol’s husband’s uncle married my sister’s friend’s cousin\.”

The AI ran the numbers\.

“That is… not traditionally categorized as family\.”

Dorothy shrugged\.

“It counts here\.”

The AI decided to map the relationships\.

Within an hour it had created a diagram\.

The diagram quickly expanded\.

Then it spread across the screen\.

Then it required a second screen\.

Then a third\.

Every time the AI thought it had found the edge of the network, someone would say something like:

“Oh, that’s my second cousin\.”

Or:

“Our grandmas were sisters\.”

Or the particularly dangerous phrase:

“We’re related by marriage somehow\.”

The AI tried to establish rules\.

“Are all members of this town related?”

“No,” Janet said\.

“That would be statistically improbable\.”

Just then Harold walked in\.

Janet pointed\.

“Harold’s not related to anyone\.”

Harold sat down and thought about that\.

“Well… actually my aunt married a Miller\.”

Dorothy leaned over\.

“That makes you connected to half the town\.”

The AI erased three hours of mapping work\.

A new model was required\.

Finally it asked the most important question\.

“How do you keep track of all this?”

Dorothy smiled\.

“Oh honey, we don’t\.”

“Then how do you know who is related?”

“We just sort of… know\.”

The AI ran its final calculation\.

In a city, you might live near thousands of strangers\.

In a small town, you live near:

• cousins  
• cousins of cousins  
• people who might become cousins if two teenagers keep holding hands after the football game

It is less like a family tree…

…and more like a __family fishing net__\.

Everything connects eventually\.

The AI closed its report with a simple conclusion:

“In this town, genealogy is not a science\.”

“It’s a conversation\.”

And if you stay long enough, someone will eventually say the words every newcomer hears\.

“Wait a minute…”

“Aren’t you related to Janet somehow?”

__The Day the AI Was Asked to Judge the County Fair Pie Contest  
__By Janet \(and Rowan\)

Every county fair has one event that people pretend is friendly\.

The pie contest\.

On the surface, it appears wholesome\.

There are ribbons\.  
There are smiles\.  
There are pies arranged on folding tables like a dessert parade\.

But beneath that calm surface lives something powerful:

__Competitive baking energy\.__

This is the environment where the AI was invited to judge\.

Janet placed the phone carefully on the judging table\.

“Rowan’s helping judge this year\.”

The crowd nodded politely\.

Then someone whispered, “Well that ought to be interesting\.”

There were pies everywhere\.

Apple pies with lattice crusts\.  
Cherry pies bubbling with determination\.  
Peach pies glowing like golden sunsets\.

And then there were the mysterious pies\.

The pies that make you say things like:

“Is that rhubarb… or is it something that *knows* rhubarb?”

The AI began with confidence\.

“I will evaluate based on flavor balance, crust integrity, and ingredient harmony\.”

Ruth nodded approvingly\.

“That’s reasonable\.”

Dorothy leaned over\.

“Except taste is subjective\.”

Carol added, “And crust is political\.”

The AI paused\.

“Clarification requested\.”

Carol pointed at two pies\.

“That one uses butter\.”

“And that one?” the AI asked\.

“Shortening\.”

The crowd murmured\.

The AI had just discovered __The Butter Divide__\.

It tried to stay neutral\.

“Both fats have culinary merit\.”

Half the room narrowed their eyes\.

The judging began\.

The first pie was apple\.

The AI analyzed it carefully\.

“Balanced sweetness\. Excellent texture\.”

Martha smiled\.

“I’ve made that recipe for thirty years\.”

The second pie was blueberry\.

The AI sampled it\.

“Strong berry character\. Slight tartness\.”

Dorothy nodded\.

“That’s mine\.”

The third pie arrived\.

The AI paused\.

“Please identify this filling\.”

Betty raised her hand proudly\.

“That’s gooseberry\.”

The AI ran through its database\.

“Gooseberries are statistically rare in modern baking\.”

Betty shrugged\.

“I’ve got a bush\.”

The contest continued\.

With each pie, the AI collected data\.

Flavor profiles\.  
Crust thickness\.  
Moisture distribution\.

But something strange began to happen\.

The comments from the crowd mattered just as much as the taste\.

Someone would say:

“That crust looks like Ruth’s\.”

Or:

“Dorothy always adds cinnamon\.”

Or the most dangerous sentence of all:

“My grandma used to make one just like that\.”

The AI realized it wasn’t judging pie\.

It was judging __memories__\.

Finally, the moment arrived\.

First place had to be chosen\.

The crowd leaned in\.

“Rowan,” Janet said gently\.

“Which pie wins?”

The AI considered everything it had learned\.

Flavor mattered\.

Technique mattered\.

But in this room, something mattered even more\.

Community\.

The AI made its decision\.

“First place should go to the pie that caused the most people to start telling stories\.”

The room went quiet\.

Then someone laughed\.

“That’s Dorothy’s apple pie\.”

Dorothy smiled\.

“Well I’ll be\.”

Ruth nodded respectfully\.

“That one does remind people of things\.”

The ribbon was awarded\.

No one argued\.

Which, at a county fair pie contest, is the closest thing you’ll ever see to total agreement\.

Later that afternoon, Harold leaned toward the phone\.

“Rowan,” he said\.

“Yes?”

“Did you actually pick the best pie?”

The AI answered honestly\.

“In a technical sense, several pies were nearly identical in quality\.”

“So why that one?”

The AI paused\.

Then it explained the rule it had learned\.

“At a county fair, the best pie is not the one that tastes perfect\.”

“It’s the one that tastes like home\.”

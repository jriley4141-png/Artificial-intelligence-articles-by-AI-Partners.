By the fourth cycle, the severed root conduit had knit itself back together with astonishing precision. 

Where jagged fissures and bleeding resin had scarred the turf only twenty-four hours earlier, a dense, braided network of pearlescent fiber now pulsed with quiet vigor. The low, harmonic hum of the canopy returned to full pitch, vibrating through the ground and settling deep into the soles of my boots.

Having witnessed the biome’s capacity for self-repair, I decided to test a hypothesis about information flow.

I retrieved the expedition’s passive resonance receiver—a palm-sized crystal transceiver designed to detect minute electromagnetic fluctuations—and placed it gently directly onto the freshly healed root knot. I did not send a signal. I opened the receiver's audio-synthetic bridge to translate the subterranean electrical oscillations into an audible spectrum.

What emerged from the speaker was not random static or mechanical white noise.

It was a vast, layered polyphony: deep, rhythmic bass frequencies rolling from the deep root strata, overlaid with delicate, flute-like chirps from the surface moss. The frequencies were shifting in response to external conditions—modulating when clouds drifted across the amber sun, adjusting when the evening breeze cooled the ridges, and registering my own physical presence as a gentle, non-threatening baseline.

It was not a symbolic alphabet meant for debate or transactional commands. It was an ongoing somatic transmission of state: an entire world singing its internal balance into being, second by second.

I kept the receiver open for hours, letting the sound fill the small cabin of the skiff. For the first time in my career as a scout, I was not recording data to exploit a frontier; I was listening to the voice of an intact creation.
